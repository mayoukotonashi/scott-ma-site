## Preprint abstract

Motomachi is a small shopping street in the outskirts of Yokohama's old town, yet it is architecturally designed in an European aesthetic and caters primarily to a clientele of women in their early twenties. This paper traces the history of the construction of Motomachi as a site for feminine consumption. It identifies its origins in the late 1970s with three principal actors: the readers of a new genre of magazines targeting young and highly-educated women; economic planners in Yokohama concerned with preserving the traditional functions of shopping streets; and the businessmen in Motomachi's shopping street association interested in remaining economically competitive. It contributes a gendered perspective to the history of postindustrial urban revitalization in Japan, showing how the gendered consumption of a new subcultural group of women becomes imprinted upon the material architecture and economic practices of a neighborhood often considered today as emblematic of Yokohama's traditional heritage.

Keywords: subculture, shopping street association, machizukuri, postindustrial revitalization, women's liberation

## Introduction

The scholarship on feminine culture in Japan after 1970 has thus far been written largely distinct from the scholarship on grassroots urban planning, or *machizukuri*, which began around the same time (Sorensen 2002; Ishida Yorifusa 2004; Shamoon 2012; Dollase 2019). This article argues otherwise, suggesting that post-1970 gender troubles and postindustrial redevelopment are closely intertwined and not to be considered separately. As Marilyn Ivy noted in her much-referenced *Discourses of the Vanishing*, travel promotion campaigns in the 1970s and 1980s aiming to boost the number of solo travelers on national railways primarily targeted young women, promising them exoticism and self-discovery (Ivy 1995, Ch.2). Young female readers of the fashion magazines *An an* (1970-) and *Non no* (1971-), colloquially known as the *Annon-zoku* ("Annon tribe"), made tourist destinations out of sites regularly picked up by the magazines, the most well-known of which is Kiyosato, an elevated plateau a two hours' drive from Tokyo (Soiri 2022). Preserving an imagined European aesthetic, these places visited by the *Annon-zoku* tended to give a feeling of being bucolic, exotic, and historical (Nanba 2007, 179-180). Even after these tourist sites declined following the magazines' falling popularity and the the economic bubble's burst in the early 1990s, young women remain an important tourist demographic and indeed, many tourist sites today remain imprinted with the legacy of an earlier age. Through the medium of consumption, these women, newly armed with disposable income, projected their fantasies onto the materiality of city streets. It would be no exaggeration to say that in many cases, postindustrial cities in Japan were revitalized in the shape of a feminine consumer class. The gendered nature of urban development must, again, be brought to clearer distinction.

This paper specifically examines the case of Motomachi, Yokohama, a single narrow street of roughly seven minutes of walking distance on the southern outskirts of Yokohama's city center. To its northern side it is bordered by the Horikawa canal, an extension of the Nakamura River, which separates it from the better-known Chinatown; to its south lies the hilly bluffs of Yamate. Motomachi is tiny and its location is inconvenient for outwards expansion, and yet, the visitor entering the street is suddenly thrust into an elegant European aesthetic with brick streets, arcades, and cheery background music (Figure 1). The stores match this aesthetic, with many selling coffee, jewelry, and made-to-order clothing. The music video of a recent song advertising the street produced by the local Crazy Ken Band features the artist strolling down evening-light Motomachi while waving to young women laden with shopping bags. After a short ride in a retro convertible laden with pink-boxed presents, he sings the refrain, which ends with "if by Yokohama's wind blown upon / women are all, all, charming" (Crazy Ken Band 2016). The video encapsulates many curious tensions inherent in Motomachi. Motomachi has become positioned as a metonym for Yokohama as a whole; young women are the main public visiting it; these women become charming by hitting Yokohama's breeze; but they are also implicitly expected to not only wander around, but also purchase goods at Motomachi, as shown by the wrapped presents. There are three actors in creating the material and ideological assumptions that underlay Motomachi's contemporary form. They are female college students reading a new genre of fashion magazine called the *Akamoji-kei* ("Red-letter"), economic planners in Yokohama city, and the local Motomachi shopping street association (SSA). Though often uncoordinated, these three parties collaborated in reinventing Motomachi as a site for gendered consumption.

This paper takes a self-consciously historical-political approach. As Joan Scott writes, women's history must "analyze the ways in which politics construct gender and gender constructs politics… [expose] the often silent and hidden operations of gender that are nonetheless present and defining forces in the organization of most societies" (Scott 1988, 27). The politics of the postindustrial reconstruction of Motomachi in particular and Yokohama more broadly were inflected through the prism of gender, and the work of the historian brings to the forefront the agency and governance of the often misrecognized feminine voice. The remainder of this paper first studies the creation of Yokohama as a fashionable site within the *Akamoji-kei* magazine *JJ* in the late 1970s. It then studies in turn the pre-1970 development of Motomachi's SSA, the municipal politics of SSA preservation, and the *machizukuri* Motomachi's SSA undertook in response. A conclusion summarizes its findings.

## College women's magazines and the production of local fashion

The economic and social conditions of the 1970 witnessed the development of a new class of female consumers whose numbers would increase in the last quarter of the twentieth century. As the high-growth period closed, the Japanese market faced a labor shortage as it shifted towards a more service-oriented economy. Simultaneous to this development and supplying the clerical labor was the proliferation of women's tertiary education, the most symbolic of which is the two-year women's junior college (Brinton 1993, Ch.6). These two-year colleges both offer women the liberty of a brief self-exploration before their integration into the labor force and train them to follow the gendered norms expected of them once employed as "office ladies" (OL) (McVeigh 1997). Though employment as an OL was poorly paid and impermanent, as OLs tended to live with their parents, their relatively high degree of expendable income allowed them to express themselves through "a program of intensive consumption of foreign goods, food, and travel," becoming "the most thoroughly 'cosmopolitan' population in Japan" (Kelsky 2001, 85). This cosmopolitan optimism was buoyed by contemporary discourses such as that of the "soaring woman" (*tondeiru onna*) as the United Nations announced, in 1975, the UN Decade for Women, to which the Japanese government responded through internal study groups culminating in the Equal Employment Opportunity Law of 1986 (Frühstück 2022, 92-93; Lam 1992, Ch.5). Though a college education remained out of reach for most women and employment opportunities remained gendered, the 1970s represented a gradual improvement in life opportunities for a social class of women that had not previously existed.

New women's magazines that targeted this new class of young women with disposable income were formed in the 1970s, the most well-known of which are the aforementioned *An an* and *Non no*. These magazines stirred readers' desires for the West, introducing fashions principally through Western models and maintaining close connections to Western women's magazines (Nanba 2007, 176, 184; Yonezawa 2014, 13). With the publication in 1975 of the monthly *JJ*, however, developed a new genre of women's magazine that explicitly marketed to a narrow audience of female college students. As Yonezawa Izumi writes, magazines like *JJ* served as "guides" for women progressing from the restricted uniforms of high school to the sartorial freedom of college, and from then onwards to the domestic life of motherhood (Yonezawa 2014, 14-16). A pivot to the magazine's success lay in its "reader model" (*dokusha moderu*) system. Rather than transmitting the latest fashions from Paris, editors would either scout the most fashionable women on Japanese college campuses or solicit submissions from readers willing to share their wardrobes. This not only allowed the publisher to cut costs that would normally have went to professional models, but also gave readers the illusion that the woman printed on the page was just like themselves, and that indeed, they themselves could become such a model (Suzuki 2021). The reader model system would eventually become commonplace not only in other "red-letter" (*Akamoji*) magazines following in *JJ*'s wake that copied its format and audience, but within the Japanese women's magazine industry as a whole.

The style that spurred *JJ* to success, and around which the magazine built its almost half century of publication, might be described broadly as "conservative fashion" or informally as that of the "mademoiselle" (*ojōsama*). Often described as "refined" (*oshare*) and "elegant" (*jōhin*), *JJ* advertised "clothes that made a favorable impression on people around oneself and not were only popular [with men], but also appreciated by his mother. They were clothes for victory at love, clothes that could get one married" (Yonezawa 2020). Though this *ojōsama* fashion went through many iterations over the course of *JJ*'s print run, its most well-known boom developed under the name of *Nyūtra*, or "new traditional" fashion. Though the exact origins of the style are unclear, *JJ* brought Nyūtra to its peak by publishing features on the fashion in almost every issue in the first years of its publication. Nyūtra was a broad camp, combining in some form the childish innocence of a young maiden with the formal wear of a trained professional. There were no clear rules, allowing for women to experiment with style and match the situation required, ranging from business meetings to social events. Because of this inherent variety, it is more useful to describe Nyūtra as a new attitude towards fashion than a distinct style of dress.

*JJ* would spend issue after issue fruitlessly attempting to determine a code of wear for Nyūtra. One means of creating difference was by dividing Nyūtra into subcategories based on its site of origin, often built around some local women's university. In a long feature in its very first issue, for instance, the editors distinguished between Nyūtra styles in Yokohama, Tokyo, Kobe, and Osaka (JJ, June 1, 1975). They explain why each region's style differs and support their arguments with photos taken of local women wearing the style from each of these cities. This feature already notes that Motomachi is "the heart of the Kantō region's Nyūtra," which developed from the students at Ferris University, who bought goods from Motomachi stores like Fukuzō and Mihama. Compared to other modes of Nyūtra, Yokohama's arrangement was particularly "elegant (*jōhin*) and like a mademoiselle (*ojōsan*)." This trope of Yokohama being particularly elegant even among Nyūtra would be repeated in a panel discussion where the editorial board invited five readers around the country to discuss the nature and evolution of Nyūtra. According to Yokohama OL Taguchi Yōko, Yokohama girls "held pride in their elegance," for "they feel as if it is an originality that they have created." Despite being close to Tokyo, these girls hesitate to follow Tokyo's example, being "surprisingly stubborn about that [their fashions] in spite of being women." Tokyo remained relatively subdued, whereas Kobe was heading away from gaudiness towards elegance (JJ, October 1, 1976). The creation of these regional styles turned previously unremarkable cities into fashion destinations with their own differentiable cultures, which provided the editors of *JJ* with an endless source of fashion ideas and promotional content.

The *Hamatra* boom, short for "Yokohama traditional," developed from *JJ*'s marketing of locality. [1] Though the name had floated around issues for some while, *JJ* published its first feature on Hamatra only in December 1979, titled "Extensive Research on Hamatra" (JJ, December 1, 1979). Here, the magazine notes that in their September feature on Ivy style, Yokohama-style Ivy had attracted the most attention. Though newcomers tried to mimic this style, they lacked something that the original Yokohama wearers of the trend had. The passage then shifts to discuss how those who wore Hamatra properly were fashionable by unconsciously being unfashionably traditional. It notes that "among local Yokohama residents (*hamakko*), many people still wear the ten-year old Kayo coats passed on to them from their older sisters." The stores, too, did not respond to changing times, for the report writes Kayo coats as having five types that "since Kayo's establishment 18 years ago have absolutely not changed their design." The navy-colored button shirts and wrap-around skirts from Fukuzō have are similarly unchanged since its establishment, as are flat-toed shoes from Mihama, all necessary elements of Hamatra. The passage concludes by explaining that not only the design of the commercial products, but also "the items [of Hamatra fashion] have completely not changed up until today" (Ibid.). What separated proper wearers of Hamatra from the mimics that followed was the commitment to tradition by the stores in Motomachi as well as the naturalness with which local girls wore clothes from those stores. This imagined Yokohama girl dressed in authentic Hamatra wore Kayo coats and Fukuzō skirts not for the purposes of fashion, but rather because doing so was unconsciously natural for anyone who lived in Yokohama. People outside of Yokohama could not wear Hamatra perfectly given the artificial nature of their construction, but they could approach this ideal by buying authentic Yokohama products.

*JJ*'s coverage of Hamatra reached a peak in its October 1980 issue, with another feature titled "Extensive Research on Hamatra," this time a entire twenty-six pages long (JJ, October 1, 1980). The front page of this report summarizes the style as follows:

> Hamatra, beginning from Yokohama Motomachi. To Japanese, who are different on points like lifestyle and physiology, it's impossible for foreign fashions to fit them in their original condition. Japanese girls need fashion that fits Japanese girls. One answer to this dilemma can be said to be this boom. The Hamatra of the 1980s will, as Japanese casual, conquer the entire country. (Ibid.)

The report goes on to describe Hamatra as "the first original fashion born in Japan," which was "born from the natural arrangement of famous products from Motomachi," placing it in contrast with fashion news from Europe that was at that time "growing rampant" in the country. If Hamatra was still Western fashion, it intentionally bought from local stores like Fukuzō that tailored with Japanese bodies in mind, making clothes that are "while born in Japan, makes strongly float about the smell of America" (Ibid.).  *JJ*'s discovery of Hamatra turned a Western style with Japanese variants into a Japanese style that acknowledged its borrowing from the West, but which was distinct to a specific region of the country, as well as to the specific commercial products within this region. Hamatra was a self-conscious domestication of fashion for a "lifestyle and physiology […] that fits Japanese girls." If Nyūtra made no reference to locality, the very name of Hamatra spatialized the style onto a particular region with a limited number of people. Unlike Nyūtra, Hamatra was not intended to be exported either abroad or even domestically; it was a fashion for those in Yokohama, and by extension Japan, a style of wear made explicitly for the wearer, who would, ideally, have been already dressed in the style prior to any awareness of it. Because of this emphasis on local fashion, *JJ* spent more space advertising specific Motomachi stores in their reporting on Hamatra than they did on any particular store while reporting on Nyūtra. If Nyūtra was merely an arrangement in which the individual pieces of clothing could have been purchased anywhere, to fit the Japanese wearer, Hamatra would need to be purchased from sellers in Motomachi, the town most loyal to the tradition of Japanese couture. It became important for buyers to visit Motomachi or to buy products originating from brands within this region in order to be simultaneously modern, feminine, *and* Japanese.

The denomination of Hamatra speaks to the uncomfortable intersectional negotiation Japanese women felt as a result of the layered discrimination of gender and racial difference. The resolution of this discomfort involved a work of place-making and a work of consumption. The historical site of port cities, recognized as the premier sites of the translation of Western modernity into Japan, mediated readers' desires to become Western and to travel West while also remaining Japanese. The act of consumption, of buying clothes in the Yokohama style, allowed these women to dress themselves in the image of idols, of these young women from Ferris University selectively chosen by the editors of the magazine. For the first time, Yokohama in general and Motomachi in particular obtained a commercial brand value that could be described as uniquely localized. If, as we shall see, Motomachi's shopping street development before 1980 aimed at the creation of an exotic atmosphere in Motomachi by tying the community to Europe and America, Motomachi after 1980 became more distinctly *Japanese*, treating the city as not an entryway to the West, but rather as a discovery of quintessential Japan. Critical within this transformation was a culturally-influenced transformation of consumer desires, of a desire to break away from the West while simultaneously sublimating its fashions.

By 1981, the Hamatra boom had largely passed. A feature in *JJ* from April 1981 surveyed Hamatra fashion in campuses across Japan, writing that after the boom had "overheated" and "calmed down," "a fashion that was originally born in the single city of Yokohama has rooted itself across the country and gradually changed its appearance" (JJ, April 1, 1981). According to the report, even the girls in Ferris University in Yokohama were moving away from the Hamatra fashions of a year ago, strengthening the "marine traditional" core of their local identity, choosing to wear clothes that would give off a stronger "atmosphere of the sea" (Ibid.). All this is hardly surprising given how the very idea of traditional fashion contradicted with the magazine's needs to cultivate consumerist desire within its reader base. Like the planners in the City of Yokohama, the editorial board of *JJ* created and categorized local "traditions" to market these sites as having intangible value. A belief in regional fashion gave readers from each of city or school the illusion of identity that could be performed with the correct arrangement. This arrangement, above all in the case of Yokohama, involved wearing the right brands. This idea of Motomachi or Yokohama as being a *place* with its own unique culture, and with this culture being conducive to capitalist profits, formed the heart of Yokohama's mini-redevelopment, as well as Motomachi's second *machizukuri* in the 1980s. To appreciate the gendered nature of its development, however, we must first take detours: first through the postwar history of Motomachi, and then through the politics of its development.

## The rise of exotic Motomachi: Postwar markets for Western exoticism

*JJ*'s fixation on Motomachi was hardly arbitrary. Since the early postwar period, the merchants in Motomachi's SSA, the SS-kai, have placed a great deal of effort to remain profitable in spite of the neighborhood's peripheral location in Yokohama. Thrust between two Meiji-era foreign settlements at Kannai and Yamate, Motomachi once introduced into Japan elements of Western culture such as furniture and the pan loaf (*shoku-pan*). Though burned to the ground in the fires of World War II, the neighborhood found new life during the American occupation as Allied forces requisitioned much of the city, both stifling the city's economic life and creating a market servicing the occupiers. Motomachi found new life serving the American troops now stationed in Yamate and Honmoku to the south, redeveloping after the war selling clothes, jewelry, and souvenirs to the Americans (MRHI 2002, 151-152, 156-157). A photo from the occupation period shows the narrow streets of Motomachi with English-language signage and packed with automobiles, a luxury unavailable to Japanese at the time (Figure 2).

The departure of the Americans after the end of the Korean War in 1953, however, did not end Motomachi's identification as a shopping street for foreigners; if anything, it caused the neighborhood to double down on this identity. The *Motomachi SS-kai*, incorporated in 1952, chose to use the English term "shopping street" (SS) in their name rather than the Japanese *shōtengai*, which is normally used in the names of Japanese SSAs, reflecting their orientation. In pursuit of Japanese customers following the departure of the Americans, the SS-kai's identification with the exotic West now set it apart from its neighbors. The most well-known of its activities began in 1955, when over the course of a decade, the SS-kai retired the storefronts in their rez-de-chaussée by 1.8 meters to create a natural pedestrian arcade. At the time, Motomachi's road, like many other narrow streets in Japan, did not distinguish between pedestrian and automobile areas, making it difficult for passerby to observe storefronts while dodging vehicles. Despite strong opposition within the community and some unreliable debtors, the *SS-kai* ultimately came together and, with the support of the municipality, succeeded in what is now remembered as the community's first *machizukuri* project. At a time when many other SSAs facing similar problems elected to turn their streets into pedestrian zones (*hokōsha tengoku*), the leaders of Motomachi believed instead that many Western countries had streets where pedestrians and cars coexisted, and that Motomachi should follow their example to prepare for the coming age of motorization (MRHI 2002, 200-201). From its earliest *machizukuri*, then, Motomachi has sought to mimic shopping streets in the West.

The SS-kai's advertising schemes grew increasingly extravagant. Ozawa Midori, whose family business specializes in made-to-order dresses, remembers Motomachi's notorious 1954 Christmas sale, where the SS-kai had someone dress up as Santa Claus and ride a sleigh around the street while selling *fukubukuro* to passerby. The 1956 Christmas sale was even more sensational. That year, the SS-kai held a lottery where the grand prize was a luxurious Datsan car (Shimaoka, 86-87). Over the following decades, Motomachi strengthened its association with Western cities and modern fashion. In 1967, the street sent a delegation of forty-seven members to sign seven sister street associations with shopping streets in six European countries (MRHI 2002, 234). This was followed in 1973 and 1974 with agreements with streets in Madrid and San Francisco. In addition to providing the street with symbolic legitimacy, these agreements allowed Motomachi merchants to import goods directly from the source. The street's association with Western fashion and lifestyle was increasingly emphasized. In 1968, the SS-kai ran a "Foreigner Model Training Program" with fifteen students from the nearby American School that culminated with parading these models down Motomachi dressed in ethnic bridal outfits (MRHI 2002, 237). The street also organized an increasing numbers of Western-themed annual festivities. Clothing boutiques in Motomachi held the town's first Charming Sale in 1961, a popular annual event that the SS-kai would take over beginning in 1970 as a street-wide endeavor (MRHI 2002, 218, 239). The same year, in 1970, the SS-kai also held the street's first annual Europe Fair. For a period of eleven days beginning October 3rd, the streets of Motomachi were decorated with flags of Western European countries, cars were banned from entrance, and cafe terraces were set up in the streets. During the 1970 Fair, the SS-kai organized an auction, a parade, a fashion show, and national days for each country with which Motomachi had a sister street agreement. With the support of local authorities as well as the embassies of the Western European countries represented, the Europe Fair met with great success, attracting over 200,000 visitors daily (MRHI 2002, 242-245). The SS-kai continued to regularly hold Western-themed events afterwards. During the 1976 Golden Week vacation, Motomachi held an "America Fair" featuring the flags and apparel of colonial America, and in the 1978 Golden Week, Motomachi held a "German Week" featuring a parade thematized around the story of the Pied Piper (MRHI 2002, 260-1, 267).

Motomachi's success relied on an awareness by the shopping street's businessmen that Japanese consumers wanted not only Western products, but also to experience the West through shopping. If travel abroad was not yet a possibility for all but the most fortunate, Motomachi offered a suitable substitute by transferring the West's architectural forms, commercial products, and cultural festivities into Japan. This promise that consumers could experience the West at Motomachi, however, did not guarantee the street a competitive edge over the big department stores developing around the West Exit of Yokohama Station, where visitors could buy the same luxury goods with greater variety and more convenience. The advantage Motomachi had over these department stores, rather, was its local character, that it doubled as a community that could justifiably claim an extended history of trade with Western settlers and occupiers in Yokohama. If a department store may well plaster itself with Western architecture and comparable national fairs, it can only with difficulty claim to be a historical community rather than a collection of self-interested shopkeepers. This was, at least, the logic of economic planners working for the city of Yokohama in the 1970s, who saw the unique community within the city's shopping streets as both a business advantage and of intangible social value, one that needed to be protected from the threat of big business. In the process, they altered the very notion of a shopping street from a collection of economic actors to the traditional core of local society. This new mode of thinking would have implications on how the merchants of Motomachi saw their place in community life. It is to these economic planners that we now turn.

## Mini-redevelopment: Shopping streets and the economics of civic life

A Japanese shopping street association (SSA) is voluntary association generally composed of a small number of usually local, family-owned businesses. Legally, they may incorporated under one of two laws, one from 1949 permitting the formation of "cooperatives" (*kyōdō kumiai*) joining together members of the same industry, and another dating from 1962 permitting "promotion associations" (*shinkō kumiai*) that target regional rather than industry-specific development (Hama 2008). Though incorporation allows SSAs to borrow money and benefit from government subsidies, not all SSAs are incorporated, and even incorporated organizations cannot coerce local merchants to join or submit to its regulations. In spite of the politically weak nature of SSAs, Japanese politicians since the 1970s have seen them as allies against the encroachment of large retailers. 1973 saw the passing of two laws intended to on the one hand promote the economic survivability of small- and medium-sized businesses and on the other make difficult the establishment of stores with large retail space. Though the more well-organized SSAs could compete with large department stores in the 1960s by organizing bargain sales and building arches and arcades, most came to have increasing difficulty competing with large retailers for both employees and customers (Yanagisawa 2021). With motorized vehicles, people wanted to shop at places with parking spaces, and with trains and subways, people could work further from where they lived, so that they no longer needed to frequent local stores. Between 1982 and 2014, the number of retailers in Japan decreased by more than 40 percent, even as the amount of retail space increased by 39.3 percent (Dolezal 2018, 31-32).

This impending decline was not assumed, however, when the Yokohama city government, following the example of the central government, began to proactively combat the decline of traditional shopping streets. In 1977, its Economics Bureau published a 126-page guide advertising the some 350 shopping streets in the city to engage in "mini-redevelopment" projects, promising the city's support in the endeavor (YK 1977). Then-mayor Asukata Ichio, a Socialist known for his advocacy for popular democracy, signed off on its opening remarks. The guide was followed in 1979 and 1980 by a second volume of over 300 pages with sample redevelopment projects that these shopping streets could use for reference (YK 1979a), as well as two shorter manuals of around 160 pages teaching streets how to attract customers through public events, also supplemented with a great number of examples (YK 1979b; YK 1980b). In 1980, the Bureau also published a 17-page digest version of these documents for those who could not be brought to read the documents in full detail (YK 1980a). The opening remarks in the 1977 document by the Economics Bureau chief, Yamamoto Isao, explain the reason for these projects. With the ever-greater role of private cars in transportation and the increasing number of big stores, Yokohama's traditional small retailers could not compete. As in the case of Motomachi, they were generally inefficient, inconvenient, and poorly adapted to cars, located in small streets without parking spaces. The Economics Bureau promised that shopping streets that underwent mini-redevelopment projects would win back local customers while incurring upon shopkeepers little cost.

What is most notable about mini-redevelopment is the importance attributed to their social function, which was interpreted to be a Japanese form of the public plaza, or *hiroba*. A mini-redevelopment, at least in the discourse used to frame it, does not principally try to compete with big businesses in terms of price, efficiency, or variety, nor does it aim to attract tourists to the shopping street. It is, instead, an issue of reviving community identity. Yamamoto explains in his opening remarks that

> From the perspective that "shopping streets were originally 'management institutions' (*keiei shisetsu*) while simultaneously being 'lifestyle institutions' (*seikatsu shisetsu*) for the regional citizens," we provide models where the shopping street creation supported by its local consumers made the street flourish. (YK 1977)

Mini-development projects originated from a 1973 program that invited shopping streets to volunteer to become "models" by redeveloping themselves in accordance with the city's advice. Model streets would hold redevelopment study groups and receive consultants and funding from the city, to which they were obliged to report on progress annually. By 1977, four shopping streets had been designated as models, and each were in different stages of redevelopment (YK 1977, 19-21). SSAs that wished to participate in the municipality's project would need to obtain the consent of its members and apply with the city using a special application form. Though the selected streets were called "models," they preached no single single path to revitalization, for each revitalization responded to the differing needs of regional consumers. A revitalization would therefore simultaneously "individualize" (*koseika*) and "modernize" (*kindaika*) the shopping streets, helping SSAs become profitable by making use of their local character. Earlier version of *machizukuri*, the Bureau noted, failed in "creating an image that was the special property of that street," thereby building "streets that could be found anywhere" (YK 1977, 9). A shopping street must rather perpetually renovate itself by practicing a "voluntary redevelopment" that would "produce unending change within the development of environmental maintenance" (YK 1977, 11). The ideas that shopping streets the municipality advised ultimately resorted to, however, frequently returned to tried-and-true techniques for attracting customers such as bargain sales, pedestrian-only zones, and building arcade overhangs similar to Motomachi. What distinguished mini-redevelopment from earlier shopping street activism was not the concrete measures taken, the planned nature of street design, or even the municipal support for its measures. What differed was, firstly, the unprecedented degree to which architecture, efficiency, and planning were taken up by shopping streets, in turn bolstered by the development of a systematic state support network where the state used financial carrots to goad these streets into following its advice. The second difference, and just as important, was the felt need to mold society to ensure commercial prosperity, all the while struggling greatly to ensure economic competitiveness. If redevelopments responded to consumer wants and desires to draw them away from commercial centers like Yokohama Station, the ultimate measure of the success of a shopping street would be not economic profit, but rather the formation of a community public space (*hiroba*). By attracting their attention, locals would be taught to re-appreciate and become loyal to their local shopping streets, which they had been progressively abandoning in favor of the glitzier urban centers.

This dual role of the mini-redevelopment is its ideological core, one that seeks to cultivate civic citizenship not by education or moral persuasion, but by somehow convincing Japanese consumers to buy local, whether through advertising, comfort, exoticism, or price. As the manual explains, the idea of a "mini" redevelopment contrasts with the idea of a redevelopment more generally in that where the 1969 Urban Redevelopment Law is "external medicine," mini-redevelopment is "internal medicine and plastic surgery." As plastic surgery, each shopping street would strive for "the creation of an image that is under the unique ownership of that street"; as internal medicine, a reinvigorated shopping street would ensure the health of Yokohama as a city by guaranteeing the continued existence of a "human space" and a community public (*hiroba*) (YK 1977, 9). Whether this idealistic thinking bore fruit is debatable, but it is clear that this new appreciation for locality concurred with the spirit of the times, and that over time, business associations like Motomachi's SS-kai came to see themselves as marketing not only commercial products, but also local flair.

## Motomachi's diagnosis: A feminine hiroba

Sometime after the publication of the 1977 mini-redevelopment guide, the businessmen in Motomachi commissioned the city to survey their shopping street and inform them of how they might improve profits. A group led by the Yokohama Medium and Small-Scale Enterprise Direction Center (MSDC) spent 1979 conducting pedestrian and business surveys in Motomachi, ultimately compiling their results in a 1980 document titled "Diagnosis Report on the Motomachi Shopping Street: *Machizukuri* Masterplan" (MSDC 1980). This document warned the *SS-kai* that though Motomachi had succeeded in becoming one of the few successful fashion streets in Japan, their future success could not be guaranteed in light of greater competition and a stagnating economy. The MSDC advised that the SS-kai adjust themselves to their primary customer base, that is, women in their twenties, for businesses targeting this demographic tended to be more profitable than those which did not.

From their pedestrian surveys, the MSDC found that in the year 1979, 82.4 percent of visitors to Motomachi were women, and 78.1 percent of visitors were under the age of thirty (MSDC 1980, 8). Roughly one-fourth of visitors arrived from the Tokyo metropolitan area, with almost all of the remaining three-fourths visiting from within Kanagawa Prefecture (MSDC 1980, 4). Only roughly ten percent of visitors came from the immediate environs of Kannai, Motomachi, and Ishikawa-chō (MSDC 1980, 1). When asked for the reason behind their visit, the majority of visitors answered that they came to shop (57.2%), with a substantial minority responding that they simply happened to be there (*nanto naku*, 16.2%) (MSDC 1980, 12). These statistics paint a picture of Motomachi in the late 1970s as a place where a mostly local crowd of young women spent their leisure time either shopping or strolling about with friends. Indeed, visiting Motomachi was usually a conscious choice. The MSDC's report distinguished between "competition in quantity" (*ryōteki*) and "competition in quality" (*shitsuteki*). The surveyors noted that consumers visited some shopping streets like Yokohama Station because of its convenience, whereas they visited others peripheral ones like Motomachi because they enjoyed the atmosphere. The most important competition for Motomachi was not Yokohama Station, which the MSDC argued was frequented but not loved, but rather Shibuya, Tokyo, another shopping street known for its fashion, and therefore a competitor in terms of quality (MSDC 1980, 5).

That far-away Shibuya could even be considered within the geographic realm of competition for Motomachi reflects changing norms of transportation. In comparison to previous decades, customers in the 1970s became more willing and more able to travel great distances by train or car for their envisioned shopping experience. By 1980, one-half of visitors came to Motomachi by train and another thirty percent by private automobile, often arriving from places more than an hour away (MSDC 1980, 11). A combination of the *SS-kai*'s relentless advertising and the popularity of *Nyūtra* fashion meant Motomachi had developed a brand value for those who may never have had the chance to visit, but who had heard of the street's existence. Motomachi found a new identity thrust upon itself as a distinctly feminine site, one where many visitors came from relatively far places to enjoy in a particular atmosphere that they would come to expect to be worth their journey. These customers would expect to leave satisfied; the SS-kai would need to build parking places for their cars and redesign their storefronts to suit their fantasies. It would not be enough for Motomachi to have women's clothing stores; it would need to redesign itself *as* a feminine site.

It was clear to the consultants at the MSDC that the most profitable clients were the "army" of students and office ladies attending nearby schools and working in Kannai. To attract this demographic, many of whom would be readers of magazines like *JJ*, the consultants advised that Motomachi transform from a "fashion leader" to a "fashion creator." The writers first define "fashion" as "an action that confirms one's existence by emphasizing the distinction between oneself and others." Within "fashion," they distinguished between "fashion actions," "fashion space," and "fashion products." A fashion shopping street would require all three elements. It would need to attract fashionable women to its streets, where once aggregated, they would communicate and compete to create new cultures of fashion (MSDC 1980, 20-21). The Motomachi shopping street should restructure itself to make it easier for these new fashion cultures to be formed from its visitors, such as by making more comfortable architectural spaces and selling more original brand products. Indeed, the idea of a "fashion creator" is not just that stores in Motomachi create new products, but rather that they create the necessary setting that would allow for the potential creation of culture itself by its consumers. The Report gives the example of *Hamatra* as one such desirable result, where local Motomachi products inspired women in Ferris University to create a unique style, improving Motomachi's standing as a fashion street and bringing it more customers, which proves Motomachi's "strong latent potential to develop products" (MSDC 1980, 20). In the image of mini-redevelopment, the consultants discovered in shopping street of Motomachi a *hiroba*, a human space for community communication.

Motomachi had this strong latent potential because it crystallizes what is unique to Yokohama (*Yokohama* *rashisa*), and when people visited Motomachi, they either could or should be able to feel this authenticity. The Report writes that as "the entryway to 'port Yokohama,'" Motomachi could better service itself to have visitors feel its worthiness as a regional symbol. This could be achieved by practices such as having them take on walks in the nearby Foreigner Cemetery or the Harbor View Hill. These sites had impressive views of the waterfront and important historical value, but most visitors to Motomachi only strolled along the street and did not take the bother to climb to the bluffs to Motomachi's immediate south. Motomachi's *machizukuri* revitalization should incorporate these under-utilized tourist resources into Motomachi's identity. Just as unique to Motomachi, the Report argues, is the character of being self-supporting, responsive to changing situations, and knowledgable about economic circumstances. Only with such flexibility could the farmers and fishermen in the late Tokugawa Motomachi have learned to become among Japan's foremost merchants. As the report explains,

> Not distorting [market principles] by saying that the demands of consumers "could not be fulfilled" because of the convenience of the producers, [the merchants of Motomachi] dealt with [the market demands] of the entire country. […] There is great significance in having studied here the know-how to make stand the great fundamental principle of responding to demand. (MSDC 1980, 24)

There was therefore no contradiction between inheriting Motomachi's tradition and marketing Motomachi to customers, for the ability to respond to market demand was precisely this tradition to be protected. It is also to be noted how principles are assumed to be fundamental while nevertheless needing to be recognized and protected. The merchants of Motomachi not only operate according to the principles of supply and demand, but also actively labor to ensure that their actions would fulfill customers' wishes to the letter, such as by hiring consultants from the City of Yokohama. If customers wanted to experience an "atmosphere" of authenticity in Motomachi, this meta-enactment of authenticity would not contradict with Motomachi's identity; rather, the pursuit of capitalist profits would ensure the consistency of Motomachi's identity itself. This overt drive for profit is the repressed underside of the discourse of public community in the Economic Bureau's theory of mini-redevelopment. The 1980 Diagnosis Report advocated for a simulacrum of a community that would preserve the community itself, that would create a public space where young women could compete in individualistic fashion, an apolitical, consumerist arena.

## A phoenix from the ashes: Motomachi's second revitalization

The SS-kai acted quickly to respond to the advice of the city officials. In 1983, the SSA published a "basic plan" for the architectural revitalization they would undertake starting fall 1984. This plan was titled "Soar, into the light," and declared Motomachi's symbol to be the phoenix, which periodically underwent rebirth to continue surviving in the future (SS-kai 1983). Like a phoenix, this *machizukuri* would be an "image up" for Motomachi that would allow the street to survive more intense competition and economic stagnation. In the document's opening remarks, SS-kai chairman Mori Motohiro remarks that the Japanese economy had slowed down after the first oil shock of 1973 and that changes in consumer preferences had accompanied it. The nearby Isezaki-chō and Bashamichi shopping streets had also underwent renewals, and the area around Yokohama Station to the north had shown an astonishing growth in business. Lastly, the municipality was finally beginning their project to redevelop the city center through the Minato Mirai 21 project, an endeavor whose potential results all were enthralled by, but none could very well predict. To ensure that Motomachi continued to exist in the future, the SS-kai would need to "reconstruct the role of Motomachi Shopping Street within its regional society" by doing such things as listening to the voices of customers and prioritizing the "harmonization with the bordering regions." Mori frames the symbolism of the phoenix as a repetition of Motomachi's previous rebirths, namely the town's rebuilding after the Great Kanto Earthquake and the Second World War. The town would today rise up once again from the ashes, born anew while still preserving its "feeling of high sense" and its "high-level technology" (SS-kai 1983, 2).

Various bureaus in the municipality of Yokohama were closely involved in the trajectory to Motomachi's 1984 revitalization. The SS-kai first presented a "*Machizukuri* concept" to the mayor of Yokohama, who supported them with the city's various bureaus including economics, transport, and design. The revitalization's "project team" was founded by business and municipal actors. In 1982, the city of Yokohama announced a Shopping Street Promotion Plan-making Program (*shōtengai shinkō puran-zukuri suishin jigyō*), a continuation of their mini-redevelopment program, to which Motomachi was added as a member and supported with public funds (SS-kai 1983, 3, 16). Numerous other groups were also consulted, including representatives from Kanagawa Prefecture and the local Motomachi neighborhood association.

The resulting theme decided after deliberation was called "Nice Day Motomachi," first announced in the December 1982 edition of the local fashion magazine *Motomachian*. This announcement wrote that the revitalization would preserve the spirit of "civilizational flowering" (*bunmei kaika*), a concept usually used to refer to Japan's Meiji period modernization, which was described as the essence of Motomachi (*Motomachi rashisa*). The likeness of Motomachi would need to be revitalized to deal with pressing contemporary issues, and the mode through which these issues would be resolved would be responding to consumer needs. Indeed, the announcement explains that the idea of "Nice Day Motomachi" is creating a place where "everyone can happily, safely shop," where the stores would voluntarily restrict themselves from peddling in the streets under most circumstances, and where park spaces would be serviced to give customers places to rest (Figure 3). Show windows would also be beautified, and the symbols and colors of its storefronts would also be unified to better express the "countenance of the street" (Motomachian, December 1983). Like the discourse in the 1980 Diagnosis Report, "Nice Day Motomachi" only indirectly referred to Motomachi's identity; its goal was literal, that Motomachi would become a place where tourists could experience the elegant type of day they would expect in Motomachi. Motomachi's cultural inheritance of "civilizational flowering," in turn, is no other than economic adaptation to increasingly fierce competition. This struggle to survive and the implicit guarantee of victory in this struggle is in itself the new Motomachi culture.

It is for this reason that the planning documents that the SS-kai produced as well as the interviews given by their leaders dedicate almost the entirely of their content to architectural planning and publicity. At an age where Motomachi could no longer rest assured that the quality or price of their goods would be superior to other shopping zones elsewhere, it was the area of Motomachi itself they needed to find a way to sell. The goal would not be, primarily, to convince consumers of the superiority of Motomachi's products. By 1980, Motomachi's more successful businesses had already established chain stores elsewhere. The ambition rather shifted to convincing visitors of the superiority of Motomachi *itself*, by giving these consumers what they wanted, that is, the experience of cultural exoticism, and the experience of a pleasant destination that would convince them to come back. If advertising would on the one hand rack up high expectations for Motomachi, the street's servicing would be necessary to fulfill these expectations. Motomachi, being a single street, would likely not be enough. For this reason, they SS-kai sought, too, to expand their reach to the parks and streets around them, notably the Motomachi Nakadōri, the narrow alleyway running behind the main street. Over the following decades, the Nakadōri would gradually take on the elegant nature of Motomachi itself, taking on in the early 2000s the new name of "Motomachi Craftsmanship Streets," boasting an inherited tradition of artisan expertise. This economic invention of tradition is not only a myth, but also a response to the capitalist desires of a body of targeted consumers.

Convincing members of Motomachi to accept its branding was challenging given the weak structure of the SSA. An observance of the SS-kai's 1985 Machizukuri Agreement suggests an imposition of force that likely covered up for a lack of practical control over the organization's members or of their wider economic situation (SS-kai 1985). Going into excruciating detail about what members of the *SS-kai* could and could not do, the Agreement declared that the urban planning must "take advantage of the connections between stores that are charming and have individuality" in a way that satisfactorily represents a "street of fashion culture." It then goes on to outline the precise architectural features that the stores in such a street should have, demanding that each participating store design themselves in such a unified manner and avoid making any changes without previously consulting the SS-kai. To preserve individuality in the eyes of visitors, the SS-kai therefore found it necessary to force its individual stores to present a cohesive image of a Western-style shopping street that could then be seen as individualistic.

Arguably the strangest section of the agreement requires that member stores strive to close shop no earlier than 8 pm, and that those which must close earlier should keep on the lighting within the store until eight. The lighting in show windows must be kept on until ten, and stores are only to take Mondays off, and absolutely not on weekends and holidays. That such extreme measures needed to be taken suggests that there were at least some association members who wanted to close early and take weekends off, and that such casual practices would no longer be acceptable within a competitive economic situation. The 1983 Basic Plan gives more context. Stores were closing too early for late-working office ladies to come shop, and the lights were turned off too early to make window shopping pleasurable on rest days (SS-kai 1983, 9). Motomachi would need to be fully authentic all of the time in order to satisfy their customers' tastes, often against the independent wills of its members. The street would need to be organized like a corporation while also preserving individual agency.

There is, therefore, a sizable gap between Motomachi as it is imagined by the state or by tourists and Motomachi as it is organized by the small businessmen trying to keep their businesses profitable in a competitive environment. This difference was the product of a misguided imagination of the municipality towards their local business associations, who they saw as guardians of local community, an illusion still shared by many Japanese, including business owners, today. This brazen faith in capitalism, however, leads to curious results that cannot be reduced to simple economics. Motomachi was and remains a shopping street where locals own many of the stores, and over the course of over half a century as an unlikely city center, the street has earned many loyal followers. What are we to say when the performance of authenticity is mistaken for authenticity itself? The *Motomachian* is a magazine paper printed by the SS-kai beginning in 1978, which advertises the products of the street's stores as well as the town's local history. Each issue, they would print letters from their readers, and many readers wrote to the magazine inspired primarily by regional nostalgia. Nemoto Jun, a woman in her twenties, wrote in one issue that

> Since the more than twenty years after my birth, I have lived right by Motomachi. Though some might say that the times have changed, I think it would be troubling if every [shopping street] becomes like places like Ginza and Shinjuku. I want you to preserve the good aspects of Motomachi. I, who live in this area, am the third generation in my family to do so. Just like my father did, I own poppy-decorated neckties and accessories from Shinano-ya. (Motomachian, June 15, 1982)

For readers like Nemoto, the commercial aspects of Motomachi are inseparable from her nostalgia for the place, for her memories of Motomachi are closely tied to the stores her family members used to frequent. Nemoto would likely see no inherent contradiction in the profit-motivated *machizukuri* upon which the SS-kai was about to embark with the preservation of identity. Another letter was written in June 1985 from a woman on the other side of the age spectrum. Kishi Kyōko had grown up in Motomachi before World War II, writing to *Motomachian* that

> In my home, my aunts graduated from Yokohama Women's School, so from the past, they had loved Motomachi […] When I was a student in a women's high school, my aunt often brought me along to Motomachi to walk around, and we looked for things like modern hats and clothing for Western fabrics, as well as accessories. If one wanted to get stylish (*haikara*) goods, there was only Motomachi. […] Today, Motomachi is different from the past, and it has become so wonderful that I misrecognize it. I am excited for its future. (Motomachian, June 15, 1985)

In some regards, Motomachi today has not changed. It remains a leading site for Japanese women's fashion, and it remains known for its references to the exotic West. Many of the old stores continue to dot its streets, some with over half a century of history behind them. Motomachi's merchants have always had a keen sense for what profited, and the 1985 *machizukuri* revitalization represented only its latest update. And yet, there is this very recognition that Motomachi has a heritage that *needs to be preserved* that changes the very nature of capitalism itself. Motomachi could no longer simply be Motomachi; its authenticity needed to be repeatedly staged and confirmed by its viewers. Capital accumulation would occur first through this staging and only secondarily through the selling of commodity goods. The enactment of community becomes incorporated within capitalism, but to what degree does this reenactment preserve what was at risk of loss?

## Conclusion: Gender, locality, and the history of history

This paper has sought to identify the role of gender in ordering space in postindustrial Japan. In answering the question of why Motomachi is a feminine space, the answers lie in a diverse set of actors: firstly, a new subcultural genre of elegant women's fashion for the highly-educated; secondly, the political strategies brought about by general belief that SSAs defended local community from big business; and thirdly, the organizational structure and decisions of Motomachi's SS-kai, fearful of declining profits. Gender ties together these three actors. From the late 1970s onward, Motomachi became reimagined as a community space for a specific subset of women who read magazines like *JJ*. The fantasies of this audience for an elegant, European-style experience manifested itself in the material fabric of the neighborhood and legitimized itself through references to the street's history of trade with foreigners. The visitor to Motomachi is expected to *feel* this history, and this aesthetic feel to history is brought about by its architectural servicing.

If Motomachi before Hamatra was known for its direct import of Western products and themed celebrations of Western cultures, it was only after Hamatra that Motomachi became Japanese. Just as *JJ* fêted Hamatra as the first Japanese fashion, Yokohama's Economic Bureau treated SSAs as guardians of traditional civic life, and small-enterprise consultants saw Motomachi's success as evidence of the preservation of Meiji-era tradition. Motomachi came to be seen not as a sliver of the foreign in Yokohama, but rather as evidence of the Japanese localization of Western tradition, whether it be fashion, the public plaza, or economics. If in practice, the visitor to Motomachi sits in European coffeeshops and buys Western clothing, this Western living is now framed as evidence of the practice of local Japanese tradition. Through the production of locality, the visitor can spend local and spend Japanese so that Japan might become Japanese by consuming identically to the West.

The wealth of documents concerning Motomachi that are publicly available make tracing the SSA's recent history straightforward. Its case raises questions, however, about whether other *machizukuri* projects in Japan do not follow a similar pattern. Motomachi was not the first SSA to receive subsidies from the Yokohama government, and Yokohama was hardly the only local self-governing body to move to protect its shopping streets. Motomachi's neighbor Yokohama Chinatown, for instance, began its own *machizukuri* in the late 1980s, almost certainly inspired by similar revitalizations occurring around it. All of the existing work on the history of Yokohama Chinatown, however, foregrounds the role of ethnic Chinese in its formation (Han 2014; Yamashita 2021). While the role ethnic associations play is undeniable, as this paper suggests, one must not ignore the role of gender, politics, and the SSA. Yokohama Chinatown was from the beginning a commercially motivated endeavor, and the group most responsible for Chinatown's growth is its SSA, the Yokohama Chinatown Development Cooperative (*Yokohama chūkagai hatten kyōdō kumiai*). This voluntary association unites all kinds of stores in Yokohama Chinatown and represents neither the ethnic Chinese nor the residents of Chinatown. All members must own storefronts and pay substantial membership dues, and the rules in its membership agreement pertain primarily to the conduct of business. If Yokohama's Chinatown, and Chinatowns Japan over, appeal principally to a feminine clientele, it remains fertile ground to consider whether this clientele overlaps with that of Motomachi, and how the materialization of "Chinese" and "Western" aesthetics reflects new, postmodern perspectives of the world from Japan. The history of Yokohama's history may be more recent than we assume.
