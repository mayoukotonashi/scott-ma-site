## Preprint abstract

Complementing recent scholarship seeking to problematize the narration of history in postcolonial East Asia, this paper examines how a certain neocolonial relationship between Japan and Europe is staged within isekai, a popular genre of contemporary Japanese fiction. Within the standard isekai plot, a teenage Japanese boy is suddenly transferred into a fantasy version of medieval Europe. There, the protagonist is bestowed superpowers that let him exert great agency over this second world, where he matures into adulthood and attracts many women. The paper argues that isekai is a form of Occidentalism in which the Japanese protagonist introduces Western modernity, under the mantle of Japanese custom, back to the West, an introduction then enforced by the protagonist's superpower. By negating medieval Europe, "Japan" thereby becomes its own fantasy, a site at once more Western than the West and yet unquestionably Japanese. The paper engages in close readings of two key isekai light novels, Yamaguchi Noboru's *The Familiar of Zero* and Nagatsuki Tappei's *Re:Zero: Starting Life in Another World*, paying particular attention to the tensions inherent in the preservation of Japanese superiority. Recognizing the narrative logic within isekai permits comparisons with similar potential narratives across East Asia.

Keywords (6-8 words)

Orientalism, anime, colonialism, medievalism, narrative, fantasy, nationalism, Occidentalism

## Introduction: Asian presents, European pasts

All societies must put away their pasts, just as they must spatialize them. If one element of nation-building is the penning of one's own national history, just as substantial is a concomitant interest in the histories of those surrounding oneself. As Europeans developed their own imagined communities with the coming of modernity, they also consigned their past onto an imagined foreign, atemporal Other, a phenomenon most famously remarked in Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1979). Only with this foreigner consigned to the past could European adventurers march brazenly into foreign territories, appropriating 'uncivilized' territories as their own. If overt colonization of foreign territories has become unacceptable in international relations today, processing the international past remains a priority for nations throughout the world. In East Asia in particular, the most well-known debates have surrounded the so-called 'history wars' between Japan, China, and Korea over the legacy of Japanese colonization (Ching 2019; He 2005). These disagreements are not simply historiographical, but carry within them arguments about the moral goodness of nation, civilization, and people. Implicit within debates about the truth of twentieth-century East Asian history, then, are older controversies about the difficult differentiation between which civilizations are good, and which barbarous.

Popular culture is one important means through which citizens in East Asian understand both national and global history. Recent work in this journal have studied how the historical dramas of Taiwanese director Wei Te-Sheng revivify the island's history of Japanese occupation, retrospectively celebrating contemporary Taiwanese values such as coexistence and cosmopolitanism while also working through intertwined nostalgia and regrets towards the nation's colonial past (Su 2017; Green 2017). Similarly, Fraser McKissack writes of how the story of a Japanese military unit sent back to the Sengoku period allows for a rediscovery of masculinity through identification with their samurai predecessors (McKissack 2017). This paper complements existing work on the representation of national history within popular culture by extending its gaze towards the representation of European history within Japanese popular culture. How European history is portrayed carries ideological consequences for how Japanese understand themselves relative to the West, normally taken to be the fount of technological and cultural modernity.

The problem is one of Occidentalism, that is, of how East Asia sets its gaze back to the West, a gaze at once stereotyped and nuanced (Carrier 1995). Previous scholarship on Occidentalism has interpreted it as a discursive strategy by the East that repeats earlier Orientalisms (Wang 1997), a counter-discursive strategy that potentially criticizes imperialist attitudes (Chen 1995), or a political tool fantasized by non-Western elites who want to modernize while preserving tradition (Ahiska 2003). The most rigorous treatment of Occidentalism thus far has been given by Toshio Miyake, who treats contemporary Japanese Occidentalism as a literary structure with

> an analog tension, which wants to distinguish itself from the Occident and from its modernity, synonymous with the imperialist menace or primitive culture; and also, at least for Shiono, of epochal crises of value, to come propose then a *return* to tradition or classicism of the sort of Roman antiquity on a restorationist model: a *return*, that is, to the inside of this same Occident. (Miyake 2010, 242) [1] (Italics original)

Occidentalism thereby involves a double movement. First, it criticizes the West for its history of imperialism, denouncing this racism as barbarous and backwards. This denouncing of imperialism, however, leads to the risk of cultural relativity, a 'crisis of value' that must then be negated by the second movement of Occidentalism, which rescues the West by restoring to the West an essentialized version of *this same Western tradition*. Japan thereby rescues the West from the West by embodying Western traditions under the exterior appearance of 'Japan.' This paper argues that this precise structure characterizes the narrative logic of the popular contemporary genre of Japanese fiction called isekai, where a Japanese protagonist goes on an adventure in a fictionalized version of medieval Europe. In the process, the protagonist negates feudal cultural norms and brings traditional Western values such as individualism and human rights, now clothed under the fabric of Japanese tradition.

A growing body of academic literature considers the place of Europe and the West in contemporary Japanese popular culture. Much of this literature focuses on representations of Christianity (Drazen 2017), and in particular historical reenactments of Japan's 'Christian century' prior to the religion's outlaw at the start of the Edo period (Tatsumi and Jacobowitz 2009; Suter 2015). Other writers have examined how an appropriation of French baroque aesthetics within late twentieth-century Japanese subculture resulted in popular feminine aesthetics such as lolita that renders consumer capitalism fashionable (McKnight 2010; Younker 2011). Within this existing work, however, medieval Europe is primarily studied as a pool for symbols that drive the story along than a setting for the story's events. Medieval Europe nevertheless acts as a frequent story-setting within the postwar Japanese imagination. Within popular fiction, medieval Europe can become an 'endless odysseys of ruthless violence, search for identity, the will to power, and the questioning of faith' (Iguchi 2011, 67), just as it can be a 'daydream' of nostalgic, rustic scenes from children's literature (Butler 2020, 229). A Japanese author's choice to locate their story within the medieval West makes an argument not only about the West, but also about Japan, and the decision to contrast a fictional Western setting from a realistic Japanese one has implicit political undertones that deserve further examination.

## Isekai: History, Structure, Politics

The word isekai 異世界 translates to "other world" and describes the genre's basic condition, that is, the presence two parallel worlds between which access is near-impossible. [2] In the most common narrative within this genre, a common Japanese schoolboy is teleported or reborn into a fantasy version of medieval Europe derived from Japanese role-playing games. In this world, the protagonist is granted a superpower with which he defeats powerful enemies, in the process building for himself a harem of enamored women. Endless variations exist: sometimes the protagonist is a woman or an older man; at other times, denizens of medieval Europe visit Japan; and in still other cases, Japan is only implicitly present, for both of the two worlds are medieval Europe. The story can be everything from romantic comedy to psychological thriller, and the hero's superpower can range from the standard to the absurd. [3] Even parodic or critical variations, however, refer back to the tried-and-true narrative of a Japanese boy bringing superpowers to Europe, and it is of this standard narrative that this paper critiques. Why would isekai authors choose medieval Europe as the setting for their work, and how does dimensional transfer affect Europe's relation to Japan?

The isekai genre grew rapidly in popularity during the 2010s, peaking around 2017 to 2019, when a new English-language isekai manga was published roughly once every three days (Price 2021, 58). The standard narrative, however, can trace its origins as far back as science fiction writer Takachiho Haruka's 1979 novel *Isekai no yūsha* (*Warrior from another world*), where a Japanese high school boy is summoned into fantasy Europe, granted a legendary sword, defeats an evil demon lord, and rescues a princess (Miyazawa n.d.). Only in the twenty-first century, though, did this trope receive more widespread attention, notably with the popularity of Kawahara Reki's *Sword Art Online* (2009-) and Yamaguchi Noboru's *Zero no tsukaima* (*The Familiar of Zero*) (2004-2017). These two novels, the latter of which this paper discusses, are notable for having first gained attention as free-to-read web novels that were then repackaged as commercially published works. Both repeat the standard isekai plot and inspired a veritable flood of amateur isekai novel-writers in the 2010s. Like Kawahara and Yamaguchi, these authors first publish their writing on free-to-access web novel sites like *Shōsetsuka ni narō* (Let's become novelists!), where the most popular works attract the attention of publishers and receive offers to be repurposed for other media such as book, manga, and anime (Tagliamonte and Yang 2021, 343-346). Such websites attract readers and writers because they are free, easily accessible on smartphones, and have no limits to length or genre. *Shōsetsuka ni narō* boasts over four million unique users per month and attracts readers of all genders, with a 3-2 male-to-female ratio (Iida 2016, 28).

The other isekai novel considered in this paper is Nagatsuki Tappei's popular *Re:Zero: Starting Life in Another World* (2012-). According to the news site *Light Novel News*, as of 2022, *Re:Zero* is the fourteenth most popular light novel in terms of copies sold (LNN n.d.). Like *The Familiar of Zero*, *Re:Zero* began as a web novel on *Shōsetsuka ni narō* before receiving a publishing offer; it has since been adapted into a popular animated series. Though numerous other isekai light novels line the best-selling charts, *Re:Zero* is selected for analysis for its thematic contrast. Rather than being light-heated and comedic as are most isekai, *Re:Zero* is dark and psychologically intense. Placing *Re:Zero* and *The Familiar of Zero* in contrast underlines how even grossly different works within the isekai genre relate between Japan and medieval Europe in a similar way, one that unequally privileges Japan as the fount of peace and modernity.

The existing work on isekai has remarked upon this unequal relation, where medieval Europe becomes 'a space of comfort as an alternative to "social reality"' (Levy 2021, 106) where young men 'triumph and are heroes in a parallel universe that welcomes and embraces them' (Cerdán-Martínez, Padilla-Castillo, and Villa-Gracia 2021, 583). As Zachary Gottesman notes, isekai is characterized by not only a protagonist who escapes into the fantasy world, but also a 'pure empowerment fantasy for the basic features of contemporary living' (Gottesman 2020, 534). This paper brings this unequal comparison of two worlds in isekai into conversation with research on East Asian views of the Occident, making explicit the political and nationalist upshot of the setting and narrative found in standard isekai works. The following two sections study *The Familiar of Zero* and *Re:Zero* in turn, before returning to a consideration of Japanese Occidentalism in the conclusion.

## A Convenient Adventure: Conquering Medieval Europe in The Familiar of Zero

### Europe as the underside of Japanese modernity

Yamaguchi Noboru's isekai *The Familiar of Zero* takes place in the world of Halkeginia, a fantasy version of medieval Europe. In Halkeginia, Louise de la Vallière is a teenager attending the Tristain Academy of Magic in the country of Tristain, where citizens are divided between noble and commoner class based on their ability to use magic. A noble unable to control her magic, Louise is made fun by her peers and dubbed with the moniker 'Zero Louise.' One day, Louise and her classmates summon animal familiars, but while Louise's classmates summon various magical critters, Louise summons Hiraga Saito, a Japanese high-school boy. Neither Louise nor Saito is initially satisfied with this arrangement, but the two gradually fall in love. As the story progresses, Saito discovers his superpowers as Gandálfr, which let him wield any weapon to perfection without training, whereas Louise discovers her powers as a Void Mage, allowing her to defeat entire armies with a single spell. A secondary effect of Saito's superpower furthermore gives him automatic knowledge of how to use weapons accidentally transferred from Earth to Halkeginia, weapons always more powerful than the medieval world's magic. Throughout *The Familiar of Zero*, Saito defeats his enemies with not only swords, but also rocket launchers, tanks, pistols, and his signature Japanese Zero fighter jet. Because these superpowers are granted rather than earned, much of the story can be dedicated to the comedic interactions between the various girls vying for Saito's affection, the primary draw of the romantic comedy.

In *The Familiar of Zero*, medieval Europe is barbaric, violent, and discriminatory, but thanks to Saito's superpowers, he faces little risk to his bodily or social well-being. In the Afterwords of the first books in the series, Yamaguchi speaks of his motivations behind writing. In one Afterword, he describes *The Familiar of Zero* as a 'convenient adventure':

> It's not that I dislike adventures. It's just that I have no stage, it's cold outside, I'll get sleepy soon, I'd quite like to get pizza once I'm hungry, and therefore, I have no choice but to not leave my room. If there was a stage for a convenient adventure (tsugō no ii bōken), I would jump at it. Men are always thinking in this way. But the basics of any adventure is agency (shutaisei) and and danger. Anyway, men want to to decide by themselves and to throw themselves into danger. Men are miserable beings who cannot enjoy themselves without feeling danger. (Yamaguchi 2005, Vol. IV: 262)

In Yamaguchi's thinking, modern man (by which he refers explicitly to the masculine gender) suffers from a conundrum. Life in advanced countries like Japan is comfortable and distant from war, but without everyday danger, men struggle to enjoy themselves. At the same time, though men desire danger, they also fear it, and appreciate being able to order pizzas and live in rooms with air conditioning. Reading adventure books like *The Familiar of Zero* therefore allows modern man to live vicariously through the life of fictional characters, to experience danger without putting oneself at risk. What is interesting about *The Familiar of Zero*, however, is that not only is the reader distanced from all risk, but Saito is as well. With his powers as Gandálfr and his origins in contemporary Japan, Saito *is* the modern man transported from Japan into medieval Europe. Not only does the reader go on a convenient adventure, but this reader identifies with the fictional Saito, who is the Japanese going on this convenient adventure. The fantasy that the reader lives through is not that of a protagonist confronting great violence, but rather of a Japanese adventurer living comfortably in a land of great violence, able to experience great danger with little to no risk. This ability to live a comfortable Japanese life in premodern Europe is a difficult-to-hold duo whose tensions and manifestations repeat throughout the course of the story.

Indeed, Yamaguchi's choice to locate his story in medieval Europe is based on a nostalgia for what modern Japan has lost: not only the joys of banal violence, but also the dignity of noble prestige. Yamaguchi drew inspiration for his work from a romanticized view of seventeenth-history European history, in particular Alexandre Dumas's d'Artagnan Romances. The map of Halkeginia closely mirrors that of Western Europe, with Tristain occupying the place of present-day Belgium. To the east is Germania, present-day Germany, to the south Gallia, present-day France, and to the southeast Romalia, present-day Italy. Many characters in Tristain are fictionalized version of real historical figures from seventeenth-century French history, including Louise de la Vallière, the Count of Guiche, and Henrietta of England. [4] To the west of Tristain is the floating island of Albion, the equivalent of the United Kingdom, and the stage of the first major story arc, a reenactment of the English Civil War, which, like in real history, is fought between rebels led by Cromwell and a royalist Prince Wales. By writing *The Familiar of Zero*, Yamaguchi wanted to revivify the tales of nobility and bravery from this period of European history, writing that

[Feudal Europe] was an epoch when prideful nobles lived for honor and died for honor. In truth, it was sometimes for the sake of money, or for the sake of a woman they loved, but more than anything else, they treasured 'proud honor.' What sort of thing is that epoch and that world, if not a romance? A fantasy is a romance. It must be filled to the brim with romance for it to be any good. […]

In any case, I have always had a desire to go somewhere not here. I like a city that I visit for the first time. I like looking at photos of foreign countries. If it is on the other side of the moon, even more wonderful. That place is something not 'here,' and if it is some other world, then I have no more to say. If it is packed to the brim with romance, I have all the more nothing to add. I want to try walking in that world. That is the reason why the protagonist is a person from this world. (Yamaguchi 2004, Vol. I: 262-3)

In this quote, Yamaguchi equates romance, fantasy, feudalism, foreignness, and the past. Medieval Europe is not simply another time or another civilization, but it is a romantic, irrational alternative to boring, rational life in modern Japan. Perhaps for this reason, it must be held as far away from Japan as possible: pure and untouched, to the extent of being on the other side of the moon. By keeping Europe distant from Japan, Yamaguchi maintains a binary between the two worlds that implicitly keeps proud, romantic honor within Europe. Only because this land is almost-inaccessible can it be 'packed to the brim with romance,' for by being hermeneutically sealed away from the real world, the travel into fantasy becomes all the more desirable.

By traveling to Europe, the modern Japanese subject rediscovers romance and danger while also recognizing the importance of remaining reasonable and safe, that is, to remain Japanese. Because the feudal citizens of medieval Europe are irrational, the traveler need not deign to consider their perspective, but can rather treat them as children who simply do not know better. The comparison with Europe allows Japan to be shorn of romance and Europe and for Europe to be shorn of reason, setting the stage for a return to Europe in which Japan becomes the bringer of reason.

### Correcting history, attracting a harem

Because Saito's strength is unbeatable, many characters in Halkeginia come to admire him, and though Saito, the world of Japan. Jean Colbert, for instance, is a professor at the Tristain Academy of Magic who shares Saito's hatred for war and destruction. He nevertheless also proclaims wonder at Japanese technology and later invents his own giant airship on the basis of Saito's Zero fighter. At one point in the story, as Saito and Louise head off to war, Colbert pens Saito a letter saying that

In that world [Japan], flying machines like the one you are riding now soar through the sky, and technology has advanced in a way incomparable to Halkeginia. Right?

Well… I want to see that.

I want to see it and use it to help my research.

So, when you go east… I want you to take me with you. (Yamaguchi 2005, Vol.VI: 257)

Colbert and Saito treat Japan as a desirable source without critically examining whether a society with such lethal weaponry would be the best one. Modern Japan provides both advanced technology and moral good, whereas premodern Halkeginia receives it, and not the other way around. Saito likes Colbert because Colbert sees technological advancement as an objective good and Japan as the telos of advancement, eliding over how in the contemporary world, military technology has not so much ended war as it has rendered it more humane (Moyn 2021).

If Colbert treats Japan as a source of technological futurity, Siesta treats Japan, through Saito, as a source of a future without class difference. As she tells him:

'Saito, it's because you have showed me "'possibilities."'

'Possibilities?'

'Yes. That even as a commoner, one can defeat nobles. We, no matter what we might say, live our lives terrified of nobles. However, knowing that there are people who do not live like that, for some reason I am happy as if it were concerning myself. It's not just me, everyone in the kitchen is saying that.' (Yamaguchi 2004, Vol.III: 116)

In a stratified class society like Tristain, people like Siesta from lower-class backgrounds have difficulty imagining societies with different social structures. It is not only Saito's presence, however, but also Saito's unbeatable power as the wielder of Gandálfr and modern war machines. Saito's superpower takes him on a convenient adventure that along the way denies social norms through brute force and attracts oppressed women to his arms. But even women at the top of Tristain's social caste system find themselves attracted to Saito, above all else Louise, who recognizes her kindling love for him as follows:

Since when and where had I started to hold these sorts of feelings towards Saito?

Probably, since that time, Louise thought.

At the moment just about when Louise was to be crushed by Fouquet's golem, Saito had held her in his arms. Her heart had jumped. Though she thought she was about to die, her heart thumped.

The time she was the most happy was when she was just about to be killed by Wardes, and Saito came flying for her. However, the point when her heart thumped the most was… on top of the air dragon, when they crossed lips. Since then, Louise could already no longer look at Saito's face without blushing. (Yamaguchi 2004, Vol.III: 78)

In the violent medieval world of Halkeginia, constant dangers threaten the lives of even the most noble of maidens. Indeed, it was precisely the whims of the monarch that caused the real-life Louise to fall from grace, and in *The Familiar of Zero*, it is Saito's superpower that allows Saito to repeatedly prolong Louise's life from the bad luck of living in a barbarous world. Saito becomes a savior not only of these characters' biological lives, as he does in the case of Louise. Saito also shows the possibility of an open future to Siesta and Colbert by exposing unjust social norms and the possibilities of a technologically advanced future. All this is permitted by a bestowal of great power onto Saito's body, great power that is then neutralized for its use towards 'good' means such as social equality, humanitarian rescue, and technological convenience. Hardly ever is the morality of contemporary 'good' placed into question, and when it is, the debate is always resolved in favor of the negation of premodernity.

Saito's attraction of followers connects with another motivation behind Yamaguchi's penning of *The Familiar of Zero*, namely the desire to correct history. Yamaguchi finds medieval Europe not only romantic, but also profoundly unjust, and chose the real-life figure of Françoise-Louise de La Baume Le Blanc de La Vallière (1644-1710) as the female lead precisely to redeem her. The real-life Louise, as is the one in Alexandre Dumas's d'Artagnan Romances from which Yamaguchi drew inspiration, was a tragic figure. A mistress of Louis XIV, Louise eventually fell out of favor and ended her life in a convent. Following a citation from one of Dumas's novels describing Louise, Yamaguchi offers his interpretation of Louise's life:

As I see it, she was probably a slender, beautiful woman. Someone who constantly compared herself to more full-bodied, voluptuous women, and even while falling into a complex, I can imagine the figure of a woman who died for love. […]

I want to support that kind of girl. The historical Louise was an obedient woman who only waited, but this Louise is different. She is a woman who, even despite her circumstances, faces forward and  breaks into a run. With love and courage, she blows away her complex and her inferiority, a spell-casting mage ready to wage her pride and honor. (Yamaguchi 2004, Vol. III: 263)

In *The Familiar of Zero*, Yamaguchi uses medieval Europe to correct an injustice he felt while reading the historical account and Dumas's novel. Yamaguchi places himself within the shoes of the historical Louise and felt that she received an unjust fate. In his novel, Yamaguchi transposes his imagined historical Louise and gives her the powers of being a Void Mage. With these powers, Louise becomes respected by her peers, family, and nation. As the story progresses, Louise progressively overcomes complexes arising from a childhood of being told she was useless by all around her and from being physically small-bodied. Yamaguchi therefore writes an alternative history, but he also stages a moral narrative where a young girl discovers her self-worth. In making this judgment, previous eras of history are implicitly decried as unjust, for if Louise had the right opportunities, it is implied that historical justice would involve growth into a particular type of woman on which Yamaguchi himself reserves judgment. Within the story, the protagonist Saito takes the place of Yamaguchi, patiently directing Louise on her route towards self-fulfillment.

It is not only Louise whose historical justice Saito conveniences, however, but also other characters such as Siesta and Colbert. By becoming a force for social justice, technological innovation, and bodily defense, Saito facilitates their becoming-modern similar to his own identity as Japanese. In casting moral judgment on the past, Yamaguchi repeats aforementioned patterns such as the hermetically sealed romance and the 'convenient adventure' that takes modern Japan to be reasonable, powerful, and just. By bringing Western values back to the West, Saito teaches citizens of Halkeginia modernity by turning them Japanese.

### Rationalizing Japanese superiority

The character that most frequently challenges Saito's arrogance is Louise, who regularly clashes with him about the place of nobility in society. The friction is evident in an argument Saito and Louise have about Saito's participation in a war between Tristain and Albion. Louise argues that as a foreigner and a commoner, Saito has no obligation to put himself in danger for the sake of a country he was dragged into against his will. Saito responds firstly that he has a duty towards those who treated him kindly, and secondly that with the power of Gandálfr and his Zero fighter, he had a responsibility not to stand still and watch his friends suffer. With the power of Gandálfr and Void magic, Saito and Louise ultimately turn the tides of battle against Albion, paving Tristain's rise as a geopolitically important country that will later cooperate in a return invasion of Albion. Saito's logic, through which he rationalizes a gross subversion of international politics by claiming a duty to protect his friends, deserves closer analysis. As Saito begins,

'It's [my Zero Fighter] a "weapon" from my world. A tool for killing people. It's nothing like a toy. […]

'I don't know much about it, but I've accidentally received this power of some so-called legendary familiar. If I were, hypothetically, some common human being, then I wouldn't have thought of anything as excessive as going out to help. I would have only quivered and looked from afar. However, things are different now. I have the power of "Gandálfr" within me. I might be able to do something about this. If it's me, then Siesta… the people in the village might be able to be saved.' (Yamaguchi 2004, Vol.III: 223-224)

Saito contrasts between how he was like in Japan and how he is like in Halkeginia. In Japan, he was a 'common human being' without superpowers or advanced weaponry. In Halkeginia, in contrast, Saito can 'do something about' a situation that threatens to harm those he loves. At the same time, Saito recognizes that his superpowers are not playthings, but weapons, and that with great power comes great responsibility. He does not shy from employing violence, but is also careful to minimize the number of people he kills and use his superpower in the right ways. Note that though Saito emphasizes a difference between his past life in Japan and his current life in Halkeginia, he does not frame his decision in terms of nationality, but rather of human duty: not just Japanese, but *anyone* with great power ought to use it well. Saito continues:

'I'm not a person from this world. I don't care how things end up. However, at the very least, I want to protect people who were kind to me. […]

'I'm terrified. Oh, I am forcing myself. However, it's like that prince said, the greatness of the thing that one must protect makes one forget about the fear of death. I think it's true. At that moment, when fifty thousand soldiers marched towards us on Albion… I wasn't scared. I must protect her—as soon as I thought that way, it wasn't scary anymore. I'm not lying.' (Yamaguchi 2004, Vol.III: 224)

Here, Saito repeats his claim that the reason he must save others is not out of some national duty, but out of a neutral humanitarianism that targets all humans irrespective of context. Saito professes disinterest in how the Albion-Tristain war pans out, even as he destroys the entire Albion fleet to defend Siesta, thereby leaving Tristain victorious by default. Saito uses his arrival from Japan to rationalize a disinterest in Halkeginian politics, yet this forgives a willing lack of self-awareness of how great power may become misused. The rhetoric of protection is in turn legitimized by a discourse of needing to protect young women like Siesta and Louise, similar to how Yamaguchi seeks to protect the historical Françoise-Louise from an unfair historical destiny. By being protected by the powerful Saito, these women are pulled out of history, able to develop free from the social norms and historical events that would normally limit their existence. The couple's quarrel concludes:

'What are you saying? You're nothing but a commoner. You aren't a courageous prince, you aren't even anything.'

'I know that. But, prince or commoner, this has nothing to do with those categories. Or even the country one was born in, or one's epoch… It has nothing to do with even the "world." It must be… If one is a man, then without a doubt, anyone must think like that.' (Yamaguchi 2004, Vol.III: 224-225)

To rebut Louise, Saito finally argues that a desire to protect innocent women is a masculine urge that has nothing to do with culture, royalty, or even world. In doing so, he treats sexist assumptions originating from modern society as objective truth while denouncing Louise's argument as false conscience. If what Louise thinks is borne of romantic but misguided notions of Tristainian nobility, Saito's claim grounds in what he takes to be natural instinct, and is for that reason all the more objective. Throughout this dialogue, Saito reveals himself to be an absurd figure: superbly powerful yet distasteful of war; acting to prevent injustice while ignorant towards its geopolitical consequences; disdainful of *noblesse oblige* while obstinately sexist in his moral judgment. This internal contradiction forms the heart of isekai's transfer, and the protagonist's inevitable victory pulls it in a conservative direction. These tensions become more evident in *Re:Zero*, the work to which we now turn.

## Medieval Europe as game: Identity crises and technocratic domination in Re:Zero

### A departure from Japan…

In Nagatsuki Tappei's *Re:Zero*, protagonist Natsuki Subaru is an unmotivated high-school boy who finds himself suddenly transferred to the kingdom of Lugnica, a version of fantasy medieval Europe. A competition for the empty Lugnican throne is taking place, and Subaru allies himself with the candidate Emilia, also his primary love interest. Like Saito, Subaru is granted a superpower, but Subaru's superpower is hardly 'convenient,' for it allows him to rewind time to a regularly updated save point only in the event he dies. Though Subaru's ability to rewind time at death makes him more invincible than Saito, his deaths are generally gruesome and often require Subaru to live through nightmarish situations multiple times. The story follows the multiple timelines Subaru lives through as he strives to prevent the deaths of himself and those he loves from the barbarity of medieval Europe. Nagatsuki was inspired by fan-made paratexts based on *The Familiar of Zero*, claiming that this mutual copying of the setting of medieval Europe is his favorite element of isekai. As Nagatsuki says,

> The image can easily be shared and instantly be enjoyed. […] So long as one knows the common tropes that one naturally learns in the process of interacting with manga and games, no greater assumed knowledge is necessary. (Iida, Nagatsuki, and Maruyama 2020)

If Yamaguchi's Europe carries references to actual history, not only is Nagatsuki's Europe entirely divorced from reality, but Nagatsuki argues that this phantasmic nature makes isekai approachable while nevertheless allowing for a degree of experimentation between texts. For Nagatsuki, isekai becomes a miniature sandbox for the Japanese reader, who identifies with the similarly displaced protagonist, neither of whom are inclined to see the fantasy world as grounded in reality. Indeed, written a decade after *The Familiar of Zero*, *Re:Zero* targets an audience already familiar with isekai tropes. From the moment Subaru is transported into Lugnica, he is aware that he has entered the plot of an isekai story, such that among his first reactions is to say that

> 'This has got to be one of those…' he said, snapping his fingers and pointing out toward the crowd of onlookers. 'One of those so-called ‘I’ve been summoned to another world’ deals, right?' he said as an oxcart-like vehicle pulled by a giant lizard-like thing crossed in front of him. (Nagatsuki 2016, Vol. I: 4)

Soon after, the story's narrator explains that

> As for why Subaru was so quick to accept and understand his current situation, the fact that he was a modern Japanese youth poisoned by anime and games had something to do with it, and for that he was very thankful. (Nagatsuki 2016, Vol. I: 5)

Subaru also immediately complains to himself that 'the lack of a main heroine in this otherworldly summoning setup was a big hole in the plot' (Nagatsuki 2016, Vol. I: 7), an oblique reference to stories such as *The Familiar of Zero*, where a cute otherworldly heroine summons the Japanese protagonist. Mere minutes after he is transported into medieval Europe, thanks to his knowledge of Japanese culture, Subaru already claims knowledge of not only the world he is in, but also the plot he ought to be long to. If *The Familiar of Zero* is a story where the protagonist self-consciously remains Japanese in medieval Europe, in *Re:Zero*, medieval Europe is given even less liberty from Japan. Though Halkiginia is inspired by Japanese popular culture, Saito does not *use* this knowledge to make decisions, in contrast to Subaru, who self-consciously understands not only Lugnica, but also the narrative trope that brought him there through the lens of Japan popular culture. From the beginning, Subaru does not take the existence of the second world seriously. The discord between Subaru's confidence that Lugnica is no more than a fantasy and the painful reality of his helplessness within it form the story's tragedy and narrative thrust.

### …a wavering of self-confidence…

Like Saito, Subaru feels a duty to use his power justly, in short, to protect those he knows from gruesome death. As Subaru responds when asked why he risks his life to save some village children, 'I know their faces, their names, and what they want to do in life. I’m not some stranger anymore' (Nagatsuki 2017, Vol. III: 101). Though a condition of Subaru's superpower forbids him from explaining his skill's mechanics to others, he can use information gained from previous timelines to impact his decisions in the current one. Return by death is rather unwieldy, however. Where Saito can use the power of Gandalfr to act upon on his savior complex with a minimum of risk, bereft of physical power, Subaru can only save his friends by manipulating other more powerful characters to act on his behalf. These other characters may not believe the future knowledge that Subaru brings them, and often disdain Subaru for his paternalistic attitude. Repeatedly witnessing tragedy, furthermore, frequently pushes Subaru to the limit. More so than Saito, Subaru must negotiate between his desire to save his friends and his desire to flee from painful death. Nagatsuki describes *Re:Zero* as 'a story about not giving up' (Nagatsuki 2016, Vol. I: 230), and indeed, Subaru requires a great deal of fortitude to defend his friends.

In one scene, Emilia and Subaru visit the royal capital of Lugnica, where Emilia must attend the opening ceremony for the royal selection. Breaking his promise, Subaru sneaks into the ceremony and makes a scene by insulting everyone in attendance. He criticizes Miklotov, a regent of Lugnica, who expresses condescension towards Emilia for her resemblance to the Witch of Envy, who once brought calamity to Lugnica. Subaru similarly insults the Knights of the Royal Guard, mocking them for erecting standards of custom, bloodline, and training as requirements for membership. For his disrespect, he is battered in a duel and experiences a falling-out with Emilia, culminating in the following exchange:

> The fact that he had saved everyone at the loot cellar, and at the mansion—all that had been possible because of him. These were the deeds Subaru ought to be proud of, and rewarded for. He’d come that far. He’d done so much. He added, 'You have to owe me something for everything I’ve done for you—!!' (Nagatsuki 2017, Vol. IV: 220)

Though Emilia does not know it, Subaru had undergone countless gruesome deaths to save her life. Because the current timeline was one where all disastrous events were avoided, however, Emilia cannot recognize Subaru's sacrifice in the way that Subaru desires for her to. These heights of arrogance then plunge into the depths of self-despair, as when following a chain of pitiful deaths, Subaru bemoans that

> 'I’m always nothing but talk! I’m full of myself even though I can’t do anything! I’m worse than useless, but I’m still a world-class complainer! Who the hell do I think I am?! How dare I live such a shameful life this long?! Right?!' (Nagatsuki 2018, Vol. VI: 199)

Here and in other scenes, Subaru comes to the realization that for all his talk about human equality and noble arrogance, his own self-confidence treats those around himself no better. Worse, compared to Lugnica's elegant and skilled knights, Subaru is uncouth and useless in battle. These two scenes exemplify the various tensions implicit within the protagonist's use of his superpower. If Subaru saves victims in medieval Europe under the rhetoric of duty and selflessness, he nevertheless expects recognition and reward, which he cannot receive because he cannot share with others the details of his superpower. If Subaru derides social discrimination within Lugnica, without physical might, he cannot force his value judgments onto others, and must instead pay heed to others' wants and needs. In Lugnica, Subaru is at once a mere human even less powerful than those around him and a superhero with a transcendental agency allowing him to alter world history as he wills. He is, in other words, like Saito, both European and Japanese, a contradictory and intertwined identity to which the story demands a resolution.

### …and a domination of Europe

As outlined above, medieval Europe serves at once as a predictable sandbox of the Japanese imagination and as a second world with its own social norms that can humiliate the powerless Japanese protagonist. The narrative always culminates, however, with Japan's return to dominate the West, thanks in large part to the protagonist's unbeatable superpower. Subaru is akin to a god because of the meta-agency he gains from his life across timelines. He thinks similarly to the player of a video game, who loads save points to repeatedly challenge the same boss, armed with knowledge of its weaknesses gleaned from prior attempts. Though he professes love and friendship to the people of medieval Europe, Subaru treats them in practice as non-playable characters who must be understood and manipulated to achieve Subaru's further goals. Saving Emilia becomes a technocratic endeavor, similar to how a player might rescue a princess locked in a castle by pressing the correct series of buttons. Subaru presumes that he knows what the victim needs and repeatedly refuses to consider how Emilia thinks, for Subaru necessarily knows better, given his repeated experiences with death and rebirth. If the Knights of the Royal Guard challenge Subaru's self-proclaimed knighthood, following another series of heroic rescues, Emilia eventually knights Subaru (Nagatsuki 2021, Vol. XV). People in Lugnica simply have no choice but to laud Subaru as a hero for his fearlessness of death and strange ability to predict the future.

Even as many accuse Subaru of undue arrogance, Subaru never considers the moral underpinnings of his desire to save his friends' lives. At best, he weighs his humanitarian duty against the pain of repeated death, a balance always concluded in favor of the former. There is a strange similarity between the game-like logic through which Subaru observes Lugnica and the 'normative order of reason' under neoliberalism, which as Wendy Brown writes, 'transmogrifies every human domain and endeavor, along with humans themselves, according to a specific image of the economic' (Brown 2015, 9-10). As *Homo oeconomicus*, Subaru's priority is not questions of human good, but rather of human management. Like the neoliberal subject, Subaru is ignorant of structural problems, and the decision is not whether friend-saving ought to be done, but rather whether it is too arduous to warrant the effort. Moreover, because he is guaranteed to succeed, Subaru's adventures teach us that no matter how high the hurdle may be, with sufficient data-gathering and analytical skills, the savvy planner will find the correct path to a happy ending, even if this path is not initially obvious. This vain hope that some solution exists downplays any examination of the abstract social and epistemological factors that cause issues to appear as such in the first place. An encounter with medieval Europe never forces Subaru to think critically, but rather prompts him to double down on the assumptions he carried with him from Japan.

## Conclusion: Contemporary Japan as Fantasy

The narrative relationship between Japan and medieval Europe is starkly similar within *The Familiar of Zero* and *Re:Zero*. The Japanese protagonist teleported into a medieval world encounters romantic nobility and life-threatening danger. Granted a great superpower, he self-consciously uses it to achieve well-meaning ends, above all an end to discrimination and the protection of his friends. In doing so, the isekai narrative turns the tables on Orientalism, positioning Japan as modern and a fantasy medieval Europe as primitive. Like the Europe that Occidentalism criticizes, the Europeans in fantasy Europe are racist, discriminatory, and violent, yet the universal values that Japan introduces back to Europe are also commonly described as being 'Western.' Japan thereby brings a restoration of the West to the West *through the medium of Japan*, teaching the West how to be liberated individuals living in friendly, rational, and civilized communities. This dual opposition between Japan and medieval Europe is tense and can even be meaningfully explored within isekai, but necessarily leans in Japan's favor due to the protagonist's convenient superpower.

What Japan brings to medieval Europe—human rights, safety, advanced technology—is commonly found in all developed countries, yet the protagonists in isekai clearly identify themselves as Japanese. Casual reminders of this fact litter isekai. In one scene in *The Familiar of Zero*, Saito soaks in a Japanese-style hot tub while drinking green tea and feels for his 'nostalgic mother country' (Yamaguchi 2004, Vol. III: 88). In a scene in *Re:Zero*, Subaru guides a confused Emilia in an impromptu radio calisthenics one morning, described as 'genuine warm-up exercises from my homeland, passed down through the generations' (Nagatsuki 2006, Vol. II: 19). Modernity and Japanese uniqueness are indistinguishable in isekai stories, and it is no coincidence that Saito's signature weapon is a Japanese Zero fighter. The addition of fantasy elements to medieval Europe permits a disavowal what the Japanese protagonist performs in practice, that is, a military and ideological colonization of Europe. Within isekai, it is arguably not the medieval Europe of magic and elves, but rather this imagination of a future Japan that nevertheless guards all of its traditions that serves as the *true* fantasy.

Lastly, interesting connections exist between this narrative of Occidentalism and what Naoki Sakai's recent book on the 'nationalism of hikikomori,' a new mode of nationalism that

> emphasizes the spirit of harmony towards humans who are believed to belong within the national community, while on the other it argues for a logic of violent expulsion towards humans who are thought to be outside it. (Sakai 2017, 224)

Is the genre of isekai perhaps the preferred mode of cultural interchange for hikikomori, unmotivated youth alienated from neoliberal society? The protagonist of isekai is not only the Japanese traveler, but also Japan, and the elision between the two is implicit and determinant. Cultural conflict occurs only with those without, never within, and it is always towards the outside that Japan's great power must be wielded. Japanese society is depicted as homogenous, and a 'Japanese' mode of thinking to be self-evident. At a historical moment characterized by the perceived decline of American hegemony, it would be an interesting project to compare isekai with other popular culture narratives in East Asia and the non-West at large.
